Before and after

Before

The early design treated an online marginal-power model and weighted energy/latency signals as likely routing mechanisms. Some pilot comparisons used mismatched load constraints and were not confirmatory.

After

Measurements rejected a universal positive marginal-power assumption. The October plan narrowed the question to energy-aware replica selection under a matched SLO and pre-registered trials. Stage 5 was still running on November 8.

Why this began

The project began with a proposal and early integration work. The current study uses Queen's Frontenac under Slurm, with NVIDIA A100 and RTX 6000 GPUs, Qwen2.5-1.5B, vLLM 0.30.0, and NVML energy counters.

This project sits in Research & experiments. The account below follows the original brief into the visible implementation and documentation, then uses the repository timeline to separate what the artifacts show from what a retrospective can only infer.

The spark

The central interest is the boundary between serving policy, accelerator behavior, and energy measurement.

What makes this one hold my attention is that the motivating question has a concrete surface: An out-of-tree llm-d endpoint picker asks whether GPU choice can improve SLO-goodput per joule without privileged hardware control.. That gives the work a way to be inspected. A reader can follow a requirement, trace its path into a file or drawing, and ask whether the artifact answers the original need. This is a more useful reason to care than the tool list by itself.

The research question changed when data disagreed

The September technical plan asked whether an endpoint scorer could estimate marginal GPU energy per request and choose the cheapest endpoint while meeting a latency target. It defined hypotheses, baselines, scope tiers, and a go/no-go gate, then proposed an out-of-tree Go module using llm-d's plugin API and Frontenac under Slurm.

Measurement showed that a linear marginal-power assumption behaved differently across architectures. On the RTX 6000, active power was nearly flat and the fitted request slope was negative; on the A30, a simple request-count model fit better with a positive slope. A single positive marginal-energy term could not be assumed across GPUs.

A pilot can be useful and still be the wrong comparison

The October checkpoint records why early numbers were kept as pilots. One routing policy ran inside the load generator rather than llm-d. A mixed-fleet comparison was capped by a curve edge rather than the declared SLO, and the single-type control aimed at the SLO with no headroom. The early 1.4% difference was a result in a flawed regime, not evidence that fleet composition decided the outcome.

The correction preserved every dataset but labeled its defect, froze a revised protocol before new data, matched the fleet curves, added explicit latency headroom, and switched to an open-loop trace schedule. Keeping superseded data and its reason makes the changed interpretation auditable.

The system and its measurement boundary

The research branch describes an out-of-tree Go endpoint picker connected to llm-d-router, Envoy, and vLLM replicas on Frontenac. The November manifest records llm-d-router v0.11.0, vLLM v0.30.0, Envoy, Qwen2.5 models, and NVML telemetry. Slurm and Apptainer provide the experiment environment. GPU energy is primary; NIC energy and cluster-wide carbon are out of scope.

The revised question is narrower: can an unprivileged tenant select among replicas of the same model to reduce energy per SLO-satisfied request relative to SLO-aware packing? The finalized plan proposes an activation-aware selection rule. That direction is a research plan, not a merged llm-d feature.

Reproducibility became part of the implementation

The branch keeps preregistration, software manifests, material hashes, raw samples, per-cell records, Slurm logs, and deviation notes beside the analysis. Every run has to identify its node type, software versions, workload, randomized policy order, and warm-up handling.

A November troubleshooting trail reproduced a silent EPP exit as an ephemeral-port bind collision and changed the job script only after a dry run. Later one-type trials showed a first-level stall. A 10.6-second stall stopped trial 616 during pre-training, before a measured cell, and the seed was resubmitted. The draft fix was not adopted immediately: logs instead linked the stall to longer vLLM compilation against a shared GPFS cache, and the November 8 decision was to keep running under the registered rule while monitoring later jobs.

What is still unknown

The main Stage 5 comparison was incomplete at the latest branch commit on November 8. Trials 617–620 passed their registered cells, while 616 failed before measurement and was resubmitted. The branch had not frozen final analysis or result figures, so this post reports research decisions and calibration evidence, not a final policy win.

The result that matters next is the frozen matched-SLO comparison with confidence intervals and the predeclared sensitivity check for trials with elevated first-level stalls. Until then, the honest conclusion is that the research question and protocol improved—not that routing saved energy.

Energy-Aware Routing for Heterogeneous LLM Inference

A measured study, and an out-of-tree plugin for the llm-d router, asking one question: can an inference router save energy by choosing which GPU serves each request, without slowing requests past their latency target, when the tenant has no privileged control over the hardware?

Status, 2026-11-05: the confirmatory study is running. Its design was pre-registered (tags prereg-stage5-v1, prereg-stage5-v2) and its materials frozen (tag prereg-stage5-v1-materials) before the first trial; trials are logged in STAGE5-TRIAL-LOG.md. Every number on this page was measured on real GPUs, and each one is traced to a job id and a committed raw record. The figures this README used to show came from a 1,000-cycle simulation and are withdrawn; see What was withdrawn. The authoritative record is docs/plan/FINAL-PLAN-2026-10.md. Correction, 2026-10-09. An audit found that an earlier claim on this page, that fleet composition decides whether energy-aware routing helps, was not supported. Finding 2 below says what the records actually show; the comparative study was re-designed before any of its trials ran.

The May 2026 thesis document is a proposal written before any measurement. It is kept in legacy/thesis-proposal-2026-05/ with a note on which of its parts are not measurements (all of its chapter 5).

Testbed: Queen's University Frontenac HPC cluster (Slurm, no Kubernetes, no root access). GPUs: NVIDIA A100-PCIE-40GB and Quadro RTX 6000. Model: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. Server: vLLM 0.30.0, the official container. Energy: NVML's hardware energy counter, read per GPU, per node. Requests: open-loop arrivals at fixed rates, with a 2.0 s end-to-end latency target (the SLO).

The metric is SLO-goodput per joule: requests that met the latency target, per joule of GPU energy. Each policy is compared at its own best load level among those where at least 95% of requests meet the target, so a policy cannot win by saving energy on requests that missed it.

The pre-registered gate passes: energy_consolidate beats the baseline in every trial. The margin is small. Three positive trials out of three is weak evidence on its own (a coin would do it one time in eight), so the trial count for the comparative study is set by a power analysis before any further claim is made.

There is a caveat on what regime this was. On this fleet the packing policies were stopped by the edge of the measured A100 curve, which ends at 128 concurrent requests. Above that the router treats an A100 as full, and A100 latency there is only 0.9 s against the 2.0 s target. The +1.4% is therefore a margin under an undeclared per-GPU cap. Its size with the cap removed is not yet known (finding 2).

A second caveat, found 2026-10-09: the baseline slo_packing ignores energy, but it broke ties by list position, and the A100s were listed first. So it filled the efficient GPUs first by accident. The +1.4% was measured against a baseline that list order helped, and probably understates what energy awareness adds. These records are kept exactly as measured; Stage 5 uses a seeded random tie-break, so the baseline is genuinely blind (plan 12.14d). This result is the pilot that set the hypotheses and the trial count, not the confirmatory test.

2. On a single-type fleet, packing to the latency target fails, for every packing policy

8x RTX 6000, load inside that fleet's capacity (job 12321478). The three policies that pack load (slo_packing, energy_greedy, energy_consolidate) met the 2.0 s target for only 30 to 83% of requests. round_robin met it for 100%, up to 250 req/s.

What the records show is a packing aim, not an energy effect:

  • The packers add work to a GPU until its predicted latency equals the

target. The prediction is accurate (within 0.02 s of measured latency up to 96 concurrent requests), so they run right on the line: median latency 1.98 to 2.01 s. About half the requests land just over it.

  • slo_packing, which never looks at energy, fails worst: 63% at only

12.5 req/s per GPU.

  • The RTX 6000 curve reaches 256 concurrent requests, so on this fleet the

target is what stops packing. On the mixed fleet the A100 curve's edge stopped it first. The two fleets were limited by different things, so comparing them does not isolate fleet composition.

Job 12321478.

An earlier version of this page read the contrast as "the fleet's composition decides whether energy-aware routing helps". The records do not support that.

What was done about it (2026-10-09, each rule committed before its data; full record in CHECKPOINT-2026-10-09.md):

  1. A declared headroom h, calibration v1: packers aimed at

(1 - h) x 2.0 s, with both GPU types re-measured to the same range. No h qualified. The single-type fleet was fixed from h = 0.1, but on the mixed fleet requests queued behind vLLM's 256-request batch limit, which a fixed-concurrency curve cannot see.

  1. Approach A, the author's choice:
  • each GPU type is re-measured under random (Poisson) arrivals;
  • the router predicts 95th-percentile latency from those measurements;
  • it never puts more than 256 requests on a server;
  • vLLM's limits are written explicitly into every launch.

Calibration v3 then passed at h = 0: every policy, cell and seed met the target for 100% of requests, with no fallback.

  1. Random tie-break: the energy-blind baseline is now blind to list order

(finding 1, second caveat).

  1. Validation passed (rule v2): h = 0 was checked on the real fleets at

every Stage 5 load (jobs 12329238 to 12329245).

  • The first rule failed 81 of 90 because its own premise was wrong. It

treated the one-type fleet at 300 req/s as within capacity, and it is not.

  • A corrected rule, committed before its data, ran reference policies

there (jobs 12330546-48). Even round_robin and least_loaded met the target for only 12-26%, so the cell is beyond what the fleet can serve.

  • All 81 other combinations passed. Both verdicts are on record.

How this relates to prior work

What was withdrawn

Until 2026-10-08 this README presented results from a 1,000-cycle simulation as findings: prefill and decode "win rates" between an H100 and a Qualcomm Cloud AI 100, per-token energy, carbon and cost ratios, ISO SCI scores, and an adaptive weight controller's modes. How the router-side policy code works

This excerpt is from the actual scorer policy on docs/technical-plan-v2, not the thesis pseudocode shown earlier. It first calculates an admissible latency ceiling from the target SLO and registered headroom. It projects each endpoint’s p95 latency at its next in-flight request count, then builds the feasible set. Only endpoints inside that set can be selected by the energy-aware policies.

If the feasible set is empty, the code falls back to least-loaded selection and records whether the cause was saturation or missing curve data. When feasible endpoints exist, EnergyGreedy minimizes measured joules per token; EnergyConsolidate prefers an already active endpoint when possible and then uses energy and load as tie-breaks. This division is central to the study: latency is a gate, and energy chooses among candidates that pass it. The excerpt describes the implementation represented by this research branch; it does not make Stage 5 a completed result or turn the proposed activation-aware extension into an upstream feature.

What I learned and what I would carry forward

The history shows a major correction: simulation figures were withdrawn, an unsupported claim about fleet composition was narrowed, and the study moved to pre-registered cluster trials with committed raw records. On Nov 8 the confirmatory study was still running, so the post reports process and preliminary evidence rather than a final result.

The lasting lesson is specific to this record: The central interest is the boundary between serving policy, accelerator behavior, and energy measurement. The next time I meet a similar problem, I would start by writing down the observable behavior, the constraints that define a correct result, and the smallest check that would distinguish a real fix from a plausible-looking change. For a design project, that means making requirements and drawings traceable. For a code contribution, it means connecting the issue, patch, review response, commit, and test output so a maintainer can follow the reasoning without reconstructing it from memory.

This is also why I keep the limits in the story. A portfolio entry is more credible when it identifies what was done, what the evidence confirms, and which environment or outcome still needs a fresh check. The public history is useful precisely because it lets a reader inspect those boundaries.
